Signs You May Need a Knee Replacement

Wondering whether your knee pain means it is time for surgery? Here is how Dr. Harris thinks about the signs.

Knee arthritis tends to narrow your world gradually: stairs become a calculation, a long walk needs planning, and the knee may feel like it is giving out or catching. Many people describe a knee that looks slightly bowed or knock-kneed as the cartilage wears unevenly.

A knee replacement is considered when arthritis pain limits daily life and non-surgical treatment no longer helps enough, not at a specific age. Depending on where the wear is, the answer may be a total or partial knee replacement. Every recommendation is individualized to your symptoms, examination, and imaging.

Common signs of knee arthritis

  • Deep, aching knee pain with walking, stairs, or standing
  • Swelling and stiffness, especially after activity
  • A knee that feels like it is giving out, locking, or catching
  • A leg that looks slightly bowed or knock-kneed
  • Less relief than before from rest, medication, or injections
Common Questions

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if I need a knee replacement?

Knee replacement is considered when arthritis pain limits daily life despite non-surgical treatment. Common signs include deep or aching knee pain with walking or stairs, swelling and stiffness, a knee that feels like it is giving out or locking, and pain that no longer responds to physical therapy, medication, or injections.

What conditions lead to a knee replacement?

Most often osteoarthritis (wear-and-tear arthritis), but also inflammatory arthritis and arthritis that develops years after a knee injury.

Do I need my whole knee replaced?

Not always. When wear is limited to one part of the knee, a partial knee replacement may be an option, preserving healthy cartilage and ligaments. When arthritis involves most of the joint, a total knee replacement is usually recommended. The decision comes down to your examination and imaging.

Should I try other treatments first?

Yes. Knee arthritis is usually managed first with activity modification, physical therapy, weight management, anti-inflammatory medication, and injections. Surgery is considered when those measures no longer provide enough relief.
