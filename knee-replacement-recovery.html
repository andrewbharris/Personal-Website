Knee Replacement Recovery: What to Expect

A clear, realistic look at knee replacement recovery, week by week, from Dr. Harris.

Recovery after a knee replacement is real work with a reliable payoff. Most patients are up and walking with help the day of surgery, and most of the improvement happens in the first three months, with gains continuing up to a year. Following your robotic-assisted knee replacement with a structured therapy plan is the single most important thing you can do.

Swelling, warmth, stiffness, and some clicking are all normal early on and improve steadily. The goal is a knee that feels stable and natural through its full range of motion.

A typical timeline

  • Day of surgery: stand and take steps with assistance
  • First 1–2 weeks: walker or cane, regular icing and gentle motion exercises
  • 2–6 weeks: steady gains in bend and strength; many resume driving and light activity
  • 6–12 weeks: most daily activities resume; therapy continues to build motion
  • Up to a year: continued improvement in strength and comfort
Common Questions

Frequently asked questions

How long does knee replacement recovery take?

Most patients walk with assistance the day of surgery, make the large majority of their progress in the first three months, and continue improving for up to a year. Consistent physical therapy is the single biggest factor in a good result.

When can I walk after knee replacement?

Most patients stand and take steps with assistance the same day. A walker or cane is common for the first few weeks, with a steady progression to walking unaided as motion and strength return.

When can I drive after knee replacement?

Many patients return to driving several weeks after surgery, once they are off strong pain medication and have regained enough control of the leg. Recovery is often a little quicker for a right-versus-left knee; Dr. Harris will advise based on your progress.

Why is physical therapy so important after knee replacement?

A knee replacement resurfaces the joint, but your own muscles and ligaments do the work of moving and stabilizing it. A structured therapy program restores motion and strength, which is why rehabilitation is central to the outcome.
