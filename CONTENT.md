[H1] » Hip, Knee & Revision Replacement
[P] » Dr. Harris performs primary hip and knee replacement, partial knee replacement, and revision (redo) surgery of the hip and knee — from a first-time joint to the most complex revision.
[NAV] » Hip Replacement Knee Replacement Revision Hip & Knee Replacement

### Intro
[P] » Surgery is rarely the first step — and when it is the right step, understanding it clearly makes every part of recovery easier.
[P] » Most hip and knee arthritis is managed first without an operation: activity modification, physical therapy, weight management, anti-inflammatory medication, and injections. Joint replacement enters the conversation when those measures no longer provide enough relief to do the things you care about — walking without planning around pain, sleeping through the night, keeping up with family.
[P] » The sections below explain each procedure clearly: what it involves, when it’s considered, and how the options differ. Every recommendation is individualized based on your symptoms, examination, and imaging — there is no one-size-fits-all operation.

### Hip Replacement
[DIV] » 01 — Hip
[H2] » Hip Replacement
[H3] » What it is
[P] » The hip is a ball-and-socket joint. When the smooth cartilage lining the joint wears away — most often from osteoarthritis, the common “wear-and-tear” form of arthritis — bone begins to rub on bone, causing pain, stiffness, and a shrinking radius of activity. A hip replacement removes the worn ball and socket and replaces them with implants sized and positioned to recreate your anatomy.
[P] » Hip replacement is one of the most reliable operations in all of surgery: for most patients it means dramatic pain relief and a return to walking, cycling, golf, travel, and daily life without thinking about the hip.
[P] » When it adds value, Dr. Harris plans the operation from your own imaging and uses robotic assistance and computer navigation to position the new joint precisely — fitting the socket to your anatomy to help lower the chance of dislocation.
[H3] » When it’s considered
[LI] » Osteoarthritis (wear-and-tear arthritis) — by far the most common reason
[LI] » Avascular necrosis — loss of blood supply to the ball of the hip
[LI] » Hip dysplasia — a shallow socket that wears out early, often in younger patients
[LI] » Inflammatory arthritis — such as rheumatoid arthritis
[LI] » Certain hip fractures
[H3] » Two ways to reach the joint
[P] » There are two main surgical approaches to the hip. Both are safe, both work well, and Dr. Harris performs both — the recommendation depends on your anatomy, imaging, and health history, not a one-approach-fits-all philosophy.
[H3] » Anterior Approach
[P] » From the front of the hip · Dr. Harris’s preferred approach
[P] » This is the approach Dr. Harris prefers for most hip replacements. It reaches the hip by working between the muscles rather than cutting through them, which for many patients can mean less discomfort early on, a quicker return to walking, and added protection against the joint slipping out of place. When your anatomy is a good fit, he can perform it through a “bikini” incision — a shorter cut that follows the natural crease of the skin, leaving a lower, more discreet scar. Some patients notice a small patch of numbness on the outer thigh, which usually fades over time.
[H3] » STAR Approach
[P] » A modern, tissue-sparing approach from the back of the hip
[P] » Dr. Harris performs the STAR approach (Superior Transverse Anatomic Reconstruction), an updated version of the traditional approach from the back of the hip. It gives a clear, complete view of the joint while preserving the muscles and tendons that help keep the hip steady — structures that older techniques often divided. Protecting them helps lower the chance of the joint slipping out of place and supports a smoother, more confident recovery.

### Knee Replacement
[DIV] » 02 — Knee
[H2] » Knee Replacement
[P] » Despite the name, a knee replacement is closer to a resurfacing. The worn cartilage and a thin layer of bone are removed from the ends of the thigh bone and shin bone, then capped with smooth metal and plastic surfaces — much like a dentist crowns a damaged tooth. Your own muscles and most ligaments remain, which is why rehabilitation is so central to a good result.
[P] » It’s considered when arthritis pain limits daily life despite non-surgical treatment — most often from osteoarthritis (the everyday “wear-and-tear” arthritis), but also inflammatory arthritis or arthritis that develops years after a knee injury.
[P] » Most knee replacements are done through a standard approach, but for some patients another option is a subvastus (muscle-sparing) technique that slips beneath the quadriceps muscle instead of cutting through the tendon. Whether it fits depends on your anatomy, and Dr. Harris will discuss it when it’s appropriate.
[H3] » What to expect
[P] » Most patients are up and walking the day of surgery. Recovery is real work — a structured physical therapy program matters as much as the operation itself — with the large majority of improvement in the first three months and continued gains up to a year.
[P] » For many knees, Dr. Harris uses robotic assistance and computer navigation to fine-tune implant position and balance the soft tissues, so the knee feels stable and natural through its full range of motion. Modern implants are durable, too: large registry studies show most knee replacements still working well beyond 20 years.
[H3] » Total Knee Replacement
[P] » For arthritis throughout the knee
[P] » All three compartments of the knee are resurfaced. This is the most common knee replacement and the right choice when wear involves most of the joint. It offers excellent, durable pain relief.
[H3] » Partial Knee Replacement
[P] » For wear limited to one part of the knee
[P] » Only the damaged part of the knee is resurfaced; healthy cartilage and all the ligaments are preserved. Depending on where the wear is, this may be a medial partial knee replacement (inner side), a lateral partial knee replacement (outer side), or a patellofemoral replacement (behind the kneecap). In carefully selected knees, a partial replacement can feel more natural and recover faster. Not every knee is a candidate — the decision comes down to your examination and imaging.

### Revision
[DIV] » 03 — Revision
[H2] » Revision Hip & Knee Replacement
[P] » A joint replacement that once worked well but now hurts, feels loose, or won’t bend the way it used to deserves a careful answer — not just reassurance.
[P] » Revision surgery removes some or all of an existing implant and rebuilds the joint, often using specialized components designed for bone that has changed since the first operation. Revision is a particular interest of Dr. Harris’s — in the operating room and in his research into why implants fail.
[P] » Another operation is not always the answer, and finding the reason a replacement is failing is as important as the surgery itself. When surgery is the right step, Dr. Harris is equipped to take on even the most complex revisions.
[H3] » Reasons a replacement might need revision
[P] » Any of these can be a reason to have a hip or knee replacement evaluated:
[LI] » Loosening — the implant works loose from the bone over time
[LI] » Wear — the smooth bearing surface gradually wears out
[LI] » Infection — bacteria settle on the implant, sometimes years later
[LI] » Instability or dislocation — the joint feels unstable or slips out of place
[LI] » Fracture around the implant — a broken bone next to the components, often after a fall
[LI] » Stiffness or ongoing pain — a joint that never moved or felt quite right

### Intro + Accordions
[P] » Understanding what is causing your hip or knee pain is the first step toward feeling better. Below are explanations of the conditions Dr. Harris treats most often, and how he approaches each one. Select a topic to read more.
[SUMMARY] » Hip Arthritis (Osteoarthritis)
[H4] » What it is
[P] » The hip is a ball-and-socket joint, with both surfaces covered in smooth cartilage that lets the ball glide inside the socket without pain. Osteoarthritis — the most common form of hip arthritis — is the gradual wearing away of that cartilage. As it thins, the smooth gliding surface is lost, and eventually bone rubs directly against bone.
[H4] » What it feels like
[P] » Hip arthritis usually comes on slowly over months or years. Common signs include:
[LI] » A deep, aching pain most often felt in the groin, and sometimes in the buttock or the front of the thigh
[LI] » Pain that is worse with walking or standing and eases with rest
[LI] » Stiffness getting in and out of a car, or putting on shoes and socks
[LI] » A limp, or the sense that the leg is becoming shorter or harder to rotate
[LI] » Pain that begins to interfere with sleep and daily life
[H4] » Why it happens
[P] » Hip osteoarthritis is driven by a mix of age, genetics, prior injury, and conditions present earlier in life such as hip dysplasia or a previous fracture. Inflammatory types of arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis, are caused by inflammation of the joint lining rather than simple wear and can behave differently.
[H4] » How Dr. Harris can help
[P] » Most patients begin with non-surgical care — activity modification, weight management, physical therapy, anti-inflammatory medication, and in some cases injections. These measures can relieve symptoms and are often the right first step.
[P] » When hip arthritis is advanced and pain continues to limit your life despite these measures, hip replacement is one of the most reliable operations in medicine for relieving pain and restoring function. Dr. Harris tailors the timing and the surgical approach to each patient, using modern techniques rather than a one-size-fits-all plan.
[SUMMARY] » Knee Arthritis (Osteoarthritis)
[P] » The surfaces of the knee are covered in smooth cartilage that lets the joint bend and straighten without pain. Osteoarthritis — the most common form of knee arthritis — is the gradual wearing away of that cartilage. It often begins in one part of the knee before spreading, and as it thins, the smooth gliding surface is lost until bone rubs directly against bone.
[P] » Knee arthritis usually comes on slowly over months or years. Common signs include:
[LI] » An aching pain along the joint line that is worse with activity and eases with rest
[LI] » Pain going up or down stairs, kneeling, or squatting
[LI] » Stiffness after sitting for a while, along with swelling, grinding, or catching
[LI] » A sense that the knee may buckle or give way
[LI] » A gradual bow-legged or knock-kneed change in the shape of the leg over time
[P] » Knee osteoarthritis is driven by a mix of age, genetics, the alignment of the leg, and prior injury — such as a meniscus tear or ligament injury. Inflammatory types of arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis, are caused by inflammation of the joint lining rather than simple wear and can behave differently.
[P] » When knee arthritis is advanced and pain continues to limit your life despite these measures, knee replacement is one of the most reliable operations in medicine for relieving pain and restoring function. Depending on how much of the knee is involved, Dr. Harris may recommend partial rather than total knee replacement, and uses modern and, where appropriate, robotic-assisted techniques to match the surgery to your knee.
[SUMMARY] » Post-Traumatic Arthritis
[P] » Post-traumatic arthritis is arthritis that develops after an injury to a joint. A fracture, dislocation, or a serious cartilage or ligament injury can damage the smooth joint surface or change the way the joint carries weight, causing the cartilage to wear out faster — sometimes years or even decades after the original injury. It can affect the hip or the knee.
[P] » The symptoms resemble other forms of arthritis — pain with activity, stiffness, swelling, and a gradual loss of motion — usually in a joint that was injured earlier in life.
[P] » Non-surgical care comes first. When the arthritis is advanced, hip or knee replacement reliably relieves pain. A previous injury — or hardware such as plates and screws left from an earlier surgery — can make replacement more complex. Dr. Harris plans for that ahead of time, sometimes removing old hardware or correcting a deformity during the same operation.
[SUMMARY] » Inflammatory (Rheumatoid) Arthritis
[P] » Unlike the “wear-and-tear” of osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis — such as rheumatoid arthritis — is driven by the immune system attacking the lining of the joint. That inflammation gradually damages the cartilage and bone, and it often affects several joints at once.
[P] » Pain, swelling, warmth, and stiffness that is often worst in the morning. It is usually managed over time with a rheumatologist and medication.
[P] » When medication can no longer control the joint damage and pain, hip or knee replacement can relieve pain and restore function. Dr. Harris coordinates closely with your rheumatologist around the timing of surgery and your medications to keep the operation and recovery as safe as possible.

### Ddh
[SUMMARY] » Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH)
[P] » In developmental dysplasia of the hip, the socket is shallow and doesn’t fully cover the ball of the hip — a difference in shape that is present from early life. The loose fit puts uneven stress on the cartilage, which can wear out early and lead to hip arthritis in younger adulthood.
[P] » Groin or hip pain that can begin earlier than typical arthritis, sometimes with a sense that the hip catches or gives way.
[P] » When dysplasia has led to advanced arthritis, hip replacement can relieve pain and restore function. A dysplastic hip has unusual anatomy that makes replacement more demanding, so Dr. Harris plans the reconstruction carefully from detailed imaging to rebuild a stable, well-aligned hip.

### Fai
[SUMMARY] » Femoroacetabular Impingement (FAI)
[P] » Femoroacetabular impingement happens when extra bone forms on the ball of the hip, the rim of the socket, or both, so the two surfaces pinch together during movement. Over time that pinching can damage the cartilage and the labrum — the ring of tissue that seals and cushions the socket — and contribute to hip arthritis.
[P] » Groin pain with deep bending, prolonged sitting, or pivoting, sometimes with stiffness or a catching sensation in the hip.
[P] » Earlier impingement is often managed without surgery. When it has progressed to advanced arthritis, hip replacement is a dependable way to relieve pain, and Dr. Harris will review your imaging and symptoms to recommend the right step for where you are.

### Avn
[SUMMARY] » Avascular Necrosis (Osteonecrosis)
[P] » Avascular necrosis (AVN), also called osteonecrosis, is the death of bone tissue caused by a loss of its blood supply. Bone is living tissue that depends on a steady flow of blood; when that flow is interrupted, an area of bone weakens and, over time, can collapse. When this happens near a joint, the smooth surface of the joint is damaged and arthritis follows. AVN most often affects the hip (the ball of the femur), but it can also involve the knee.
[H4] » What causes it
[P] » Sometimes no cause is found, but recognized risk factors include:
[LI] » Long-term use of corticosteroid medication
[LI] » Significant alcohol use
[LI] » A prior hip fracture or dislocation that disrupted blood flow
[LI] » Certain medical conditions, such as sickle cell disease and lupus
[P] » In its earliest stages AVN can be silent and is sometimes discovered on imaging done for another reason. As it progresses, it typically causes pain with weight-bearing — in the groin or hip, or in the knee — that steadily worsens. An MRI is the most sensitive test and can detect AVN before it is visible on an ordinary X-ray.
[P] » Treatment depends on how early the condition is caught and whether the bone has already collapsed. Caught early, before collapse, joint-preserving options may be considered, and Dr. Harris will review your imaging and stage of disease with you.
[P] » Once the bone has collapsed and the joint surface is damaged, hip or knee replacement is the most dependable way to relieve pain and return you to activity. Dr. Harris will help you understand where you are in the disease and recommend the approach best suited to your situation.
[SUMMARY] » When a Hip or Knee Replacement Fails
[P] » Modern hip and knee replacements are durable, and the great majority last for many years — often two decades or more. But no implant lasts forever, and occasionally a replacement can wear out or run into trouble sooner. When that happens, revision surgery can identify the problem and rebuild the joint.
[H4] » Why a replacement can fail
[LI] » Wear and loosening. Over years of use, an implant can loosen from the bone, or the bearing surface can wear down.
[LI] » Infection. Bacteria can settle on a replacement, causing pain, swelling, and warmth — sometimes years after surgery.
[LI] » Instability or dislocation. The joint can become unstable or, in the hip, slip out of place.
[LI] » Periprosthetic fracture. A break in the bone around the implant, often after a fall or in bone weakened by osteoporosis.
[LI] » Stiffness or persistent pain that limits how well the joint works.
[H4] » Signs to watch for
[P] » New or worsening pain, swelling, a sense that the joint is giving way or clunking, decreased motion, or new difficulty bearing weight are all reasons to be evaluated. Many of these problems are very treatable, especially when addressed early.
[P] » Revision surgery is more complex than a first-time replacement, and finding the cause is the essential first step. Dr. Harris evaluates the joint with a careful examination, X-rays, laboratory tests, and, when needed, advanced imaging.
[P] » The revision is then planned around the specific problem — exchanging worn or loose components, treating infection (sometimes in stages), restoring stability, or repairing bone. Much of Dr. Harris’s research is devoted to understanding why replacements fail and how to make them last longer, and that work directly informs the care he provides to patients facing these problems.
[P] » This information is provided for general education and is not a substitute for a personal evaluation. Every patient is different; the right diagnosis and treatment plan can only be determined through an individual consultation.

[H3] » Preventing Infection
[P] » Infection is one of the most serious complications of joint replacement. Dr. Harris’s work looks at who is most at risk — including the bacteria patients carry on their skin and the medical conditions that raise risk — so that steps can be taken before surgery to help prevent it.
