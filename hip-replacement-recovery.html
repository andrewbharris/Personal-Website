Hip Replacement Recovery: What to Expect

A realistic look at the expected recovery after a modern hip replacement

Recovery from a modern hip replacement is faster than most people expect. Most patients are up and walking with help the same day, home on the same day of surgery1, and back to most daily activities within two to three months (or less)2.

The problems patients most commonly run into early are bruising, stiffness, pain, and disturbed sleep. Patients who take an active role in their own recovery tend to have better outcomes, and going in with realistic expectations has been shown to matter as well.

A typical timeline for most patients

  • Day of surgerystand and take steps with assistance
  • First 1 to 2 weekswalking with a walker or cane, focus on comfort and safe movement
  • 2 to 6 weeksless need for walking aids, gradual return to light activity and driving
  • 6 to 12 weeksmost daily activities resume, strength and endurance keep building
  • A year and beyondimprovement can continue well past the first year, particularly for patients who had significant weakness or stiffness before surgery

The first two weeks

The goal early on is comfort, safe walking, and protecting the incision. Patients are instructed to use a walker at first and move to a cane as balance and confidence return. Dr. Harris asks patients to stay on a walking aid for a set period rather than giving it up as soon as it feels possible. The main risk in the first few weeks is a fall, and a walker or cane is the simplest way to prevent one. Most patients only need one for a short time. In one study that surveyed patients every week after surgery, 91% were using a walking aid in the first week and 15% still were at six weeks.3

Swelling is very common after hip replacement, though a number of steps are taken during and after surgery to limit it. When it does occur, gravity pulls fluid down the leg, so it can show up in the thigh and sometimes into the calf and ankle. Sleep is often the hardest part of the first two weeks, although most people find a position that works to sleep as much as they can through the night.

Movements to limit early on

There are usually some movements to avoid or limit for roughly six to eight weeks after surgery while the soft tissues heal. What those are depends on the specific operation you had, so the instructions you are given may not match what a friend was told after their hip replacement. Dr. Harris’ team will go through your specific post-surgery movement limitations before you leave.

Rehabilitation

Physical therapy

How much formal therapy a patient needs after hip replacement surgery varies. Some patients do best with outpatient physical therapy, meaning going to a PT office for scheduled appointments and sessions. Others do just as well with a self-directed home exercise program. Both of these options may work for different individual patients. The option that makes sense depends on the patient and on the exact surgery performed. A randomized trial found that formal physical therapy is not required after a routine hip replacement, and a review of seven trials found no meaningful difference in strength, function, or quality of life between supervised therapy and a home program.4,5

What does not vary is that some amount of effort is required by the patient in order to achieve a full recovery. Regular, purposeful walking is the foundation and most important aspect of recovery, and the exercises you are given are there to rebuild the muscle that may have atrophied after years of dealing with arthritis or a period of weakness and lack of activity immediately after surgery.

Pain control

Dr. Harris uses a modern approach to pain control after hip replacement that minimizes narcotics such as oxycodone and Percocet, and instead relies on a multimodal regimen, meaning several different medications that work in different ways and are taken together.

There are good reasons to keep narcotics to a minimum. They cause nausea, constipation, drowsiness, and mental fog. They also disrupt sleep rather than improving it. Narcotic medications make it harder to get up and walk, which is the single most important thing a patient can do in the first weeks. They also carry a risk of dependence (although this risk is low in most patients). Several non-narcotic medications used together often control pain at least as well, with far fewer of those effects.

Minimizing narcotics does not mean accepting more pain. Both goals are very important, and keeping pain under control is a central part of the plan rather than something traded away.

Not everything that helps is a medication. Ice, elevating the leg, and changing position regularly all reduce pain and swelling, and they work alongside the medications rather than instead of them.

Some patients need no opioid medication at all. Most who do need it for one to two weeks, and then stop. Pain that is well controlled early makes it easier to walk, and walking is what drives the rest of recovery.

Driving

Two things determine when you can drive again after hip replacement surgery.

The first is that you cannot drive if you are still taking narcotic pain medication. The second is whether you can safely control the car, which means moving your foot to the brake without hesitating and getting in and out without difficulty.

A left hip usually allows a return to driving sooner than a right hip, since an automatic transmission does not require you to use a clutch with your left leg. Most patients are driving again within a few weeks. In one study that followed patients week by week, 60% were driving by four weeks and 77% by six weeks.3 Dr. Harris will give you guidance based on your progress and which hip was operated on.

Going back to work

Desk work is usually possible within two to four weeks, and often sooner if you can work from home and change position when you need to. Physically demanding work takes longer to return to and is best done slowly over time. The exact time you could possibly return to a more physically demanding job depends on how much standing, lifting, and climbing the job involves.

What to Watch For

What is normal, and what is not

There may be some small speedbumps along the way, such as bruising and discomfort. For many patients these are minimal. Unplanned trips to an emergency department after hip replacement are uncommon, and when they do happen they are usually in the first two weeks, most often for pain or swelling.6

A few things are not a normal part of the recovery process, and these specific issues may need attention rather than waiting for your next appointment:

  • Sudden severe pain, or a sudden inability to put weight through the leg
  • Calf pain, or severe swelling of one leg that is clearly worse than the other
  • Shortness of breath

Shortness of breath, especially if it comes on suddenly or with chest pain, is an emergency.

Long-term activity

Most patients have no lifetime restrictions after a hip replacement. Walking, swimming, cycling, golf, hiking, and doubles tennis are all reasonable and many patients return to these activities after surgery. Staying active is good for you and good for the muscles surrounding your hip replacement.

High-impact repetitive activity is a more open question. Whether distance running or extreme sports shorten the life of a hip replacement is still debated among surgeons, and the honest answer is that we do not have a definitive one. Dr. Harris discusses this individually rather than issuing a blanket rule.

Does the surgical approach change recovery?

The anterior approach and the STAR approach are both modern, muscle-sparing techniques to perform hip replacement surgery, and patients often find the first few weeks easier than they expect. By three months the difference between approaches matters much less than the work you put in and how your hip was doing before surgery. Patients can do exceptionally well with both approaches. A study using motion sensors found small differences in walking between the anterior and posterior approaches in the first month, and no measurable difference by four months.7 The STAR approach preserves the piriformis and quadratus femoris muscles, and has been reported to produce excellent early outcomes.8,9

Follow-Up

What happens at your follow-up visits

You will be seen in the office after surgery to check the incision, review your progress, and take an X-ray of the new hip. Later visits space out as you reach your final level of long-term recovery. Over a period of years, a hip replacement is worth checking periodically even when it feels perfect, because problems are easier to address when they are found early.

Frequently asked questions

How long does hip replacement recovery take?

Most patients walk with assistance the same day or the next day, are moving comfortably around the house within a few weeks, and return to most normal activities within about two to three months. It can take up to a year to feel the full benefit of the new hip, particularly if you had more stiffness or weakness prior to hip replacement surgery.

When can I walk after hip replacement?

Most patients stand and take steps with assistance the day of surgery. Many use a walker or cane for the first few weeks, then progress to walking without aids as comfort and strength return.

When can I drive after hip replacement?

Not while you are taking opioid pain medication, and not until you can move your foot to the brake without hesitating. A left hip usually allows driving sooner than a right hip. Most patients are back to driving within a few weeks.

Is hip replacement outpatient or same-day?

For many healthy patients, hip replacement can be done as an outpatient, same-day procedure with recovery continued at home, including with the anterior and STAR approaches. Whether that is right for you depends on your age, other health conditions, and home setup.

Will I need physical therapy after a hip replacement?

It depends. Some patients do best with formal outpatient physical therapy. Others do just as well with a self-directed home exercise program. Both approaches work, and which one makes sense depends on the patient and the exact surgery performed.

How long will I need pain medication?

Dr. Harris uses a multimodal approach that minimizes narcotics, combining several non-narcotic medications that work in different ways, along with ice, elevation, and regular movement. Some patients need no opioid medication at all. Most who do need it for one to two weeks, and then stop.

When can I go back to work?

Desk work is usually possible within two to four weeks, and often sooner if you can work from home. Physically demanding work takes longer, depending on how much standing, lifting, and climbing is involved.

Are there movements I should avoid after a hip replacement?

Usually there are some movements to avoid or limit for about six to eight weeks while the soft tissues heal. What those are depends on the specific operation you had, so follow the instructions you are given.

Can I run or play sports after a hip replacement?

Most patients have no lifetime restrictions. Walking, swimming, cycling, golf, and hiking are all reasonable and encouraged. Whether high-impact repetitive activity such as distance running shortens the life of a hip replacement is still debated among surgeons, so it is worth discussing individually with Dr. Harris regarding the activities that are important to you and the exact surgery you had performed.

How long does a hip replacement last?

Modern hip replacements are built to last. Registry data covering nearly two million hips shows more than nine in ten still in place at twenty years, and about the same at thirty. In practical terms, the large majority of hip replacements are still working three decades later, and many patients will never need another operation on that hip.
